Why Joinery Matters More Than You Think

The joints holding your furniture together are invisible in the finished piece, but they determine whether it lasts a decade or a century. Most people never think about joinery. They see the surface — the grain, the colour, the shape. But underneath, the way the wood is joined is what separates furniture that endures from furniture that eventually fails.

In my workshop, I use traditional joinery techniques that have been refined over centuries. Here is why that matters, and what you should know.

What is joinery?

Joinery is the method of connecting two pieces of wood without relying on nails, screws, or metal fixings. The wood itself is shaped so that one piece locks into another. The joint is the structure. When done well, it is stronger than the wood around it.

There are dozens of joint types, each suited to different applications. The ones I use most frequently are mortise and tenon, dovetails, and finger joints.

Mortise and tenon

This is the workhorse of furniture joinery. A tenon — a projecting piece of wood — fits snugly into a mortise — a corresponding slot. It is the joint you will find in most of my table legs, bed frames, and structural pieces. The beauty of mortise and tenon is its simplicity and its strength. When properly fitted and glued, it creates a bond that can carry enormous weight and resist years of lateral stress.

Dovetails

Dovetails are the joint people recognise. The interlocking fan-shaped tails and pins are distinctive and impossible to fake. I use them primarily in drawer construction, boxes, and ring boxes. They resist being pulled apart in one direction, which makes them ideal for drawers that are opened and closed thousands of times over a lifetime. Hand-cut dovetails also carry a visual quality that machine-cut versions cannot replicate — slight imperfections that prove the piece was made by hand.

Why not just use screws?

Screws and nails are metal. Wood is organic. Over time, wood moves — it expands and contracts with changes in humidity and temperature. Metal does not. This creates stress points where the two materials meet. Eventually, screw holes loosen, joints develop play, and the piece starts to feel less solid.

Traditional joinery moves with the wood because it is made of the same material. The joint breathes. It accommodates seasonal movement without losing structural integrity. This is why properly jointed furniture can last for generations while flat-pack furniture rarely survives a single house move.

The time investment

Hand-cut joints take longer than machine-cut alternatives. A single mortise and tenon joint, cut and fitted by hand, can take an hour or more. A set of dovetails for a drawer can take half a day. This is time that goes into every piece I make, and it is time that pays for itself over the life of the furniture.

When you commission a piece from me, the joinery is not something you will see every day. But it is the reason the piece still feels solid in twenty years. It is the reason it can be handed down. And it is the reason I'll never take shortcuts with it.
