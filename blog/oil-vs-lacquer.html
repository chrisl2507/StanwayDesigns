Oil vs Lacquer: Choosing the Right Finish

The finish you apply to timber changes how it looks, feels, and ages. It is the final decision in any commission, and it is one that affects the piece every single day. Most clients ask about this during an initial consultation, and the answer is always the same: it depends on how you want to live with the furniture.

Here is a straightforward comparison of the two finishes I use most — natural oil and lacquer — so you can make an informed choice.

Natural oil

Oil penetrates the wood rather than sitting on top of it. It soaks into the grain, enhancing the natural colour and texture without creating a surface coating. The result is a finish that looks and feels like real wood — because it is. There is no plastic layer between your hand and the timber.

Oil finishes bring out the depth in grain patterns. They give the wood a warm, matte appearance that photographs beautifully and feels tactile. Over time, an oiled surface develops a patina — a subtle change in tone that comes from use. Scratches and marks blend into the surface rather than standing out.

The trade-off is maintenance. An oiled surface needs re-oiling once or twice a year, depending on use. This is simple — apply oil with a cloth, leave it for twenty minutes, wipe off the excess. It takes less time than cleaning the piece. But it does require attention.

Lacquer

Lacquer creates a hard, protective film on the surface of the wood. It seals the timber completely, making it resistant to moisture, stains, and heat marks. Modern lacquers are available in matte, satin, and gloss finishes, so the visual result can range from barely visible to mirror-like.

A lacquered surface is low-maintenance. It wipes clean easily and does not require regular re-application. For kitchen tables, surfaces used by children, or pieces in high-traffic areas, lacquer provides practical protection that oil cannot match.

The trade-off is feel and ageing. Lacquer sits on top of the wood, so the surface feels slightly different to the touch — smoother, more uniform, less organic. When lacquer is damaged — a deep scratch or a chip — the repair is more involved than simply re-oiling. The damaged area needs to be sanded back and re-coated, and matching the finish precisely requires skill.

Which should you choose?

If you value the tactile quality of natural wood and you enjoy the idea of a piece that evolves with use, choose oil. It rewards attention and it ages beautifully. Most of my dining tables and statement pieces are finished with natural oil.

If you need durability and low maintenance — particularly for surfaces that see daily heavy use like kitchen worktops or children's desks — lacquer is the practical choice. It protects the wood and requires less ongoing care.

There is no wrong answer. Both finishes protect the timber and both look excellent when properly applied. The decision is about how you want to interact with the piece over time. I'm always happy to show you samples of both finishes on your chosen timber so you can see and feel the difference before committing.
